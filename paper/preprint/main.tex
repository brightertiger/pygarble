\begin{abstract}
Text that reaches a language model, a survey database or a training corpus
is not always text. Keyboard mashing, random strings and placeholder
answers are common, and the usual detectors for them are either trained
classifiers with model downloads or hand-written rules that are rarely
measured. pygarble is a Python library with no runtime dependencies that
screens text with \nstrategies{} rule-based and statistical strategies
combined into named profiles. We evaluate it on two public English short-text datasets, a
Kaggle collection of \nA{} free-text survey responses labelled as junk and
a Hugging Face collection of \nB{} English rows that pairs \nBpos{} random
strings with \nBneg{} tweets, against a character Markov-chain gibberish
detector and a language identifier, every method at its own default
decision. Out of the box, the \code{english\_fusion} profile captures
\respct{english_fusion}{A_capture}\,\% of the survey junk and recalls
\respct{english_fusion}{B_recall}\,\% of the random strings at a precision
of \res{english_fusion}{B_precision}, from a \mb{english_fusion}\,MB
install that processes a thousand short texts in \ms{english_fusion}\,ms;
no baseline captures more survey junk while also flagging fewer tweets.
A small labelled sample shifts the trade-off: a weight for every strategy
and a threshold learned with Optuna from \tuningpct\,\% of the rows, in
about \tuningseconds{} seconds of search, cuts the false-positive rate on
tweets from \tmeanpct{english_fusion}{B_fpr}\,\% to
\tmeanpct{tuned}{B_fpr}\,\% and raises precision from
\tmean{english_fusion}{B_precision} to \tmean{tuned}{B_precision} on the
held-out \heldoutpct\,\%, with capture about level
(\tmeanpct{tuned}{A_capture}\,\% against
\tmeanpct{english_fusion}{A_capture}\,\%) and recall lower
(\tmeanpct{tuned}{B_recall}\,\% against
\tmeanpct{english_fusion}{B_recall}\,\%; means over \tuningdraws{} draws).
The learned weights are interpretable at the extremes: word lookup gets
full weight in every draw, and the two encoding checks, which fire on
tweets this dataset labels clean, get almost none. A
dependency-free rule ensemble is therefore competitive on real junk as
shipped and adapts to a domain in seconds, with every decision
explainable strategy by strategy. Code, per-row predictions and a
verification script are released so that every number can be recomputed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Free-text fields collect junk. Respondents mash the keyboard to reach the
next page of a survey, bots submit random strings to sign-up forms, and
scraped text carries encoding damage. Each of these costs something
downstream: a coder reads it, a model is trained on it, or an analysis
counts it as an answer. The problem has become more visible with large
language models (LLMs), where unreadable input wastes tokens, degrades
retrieval, and in guardrail settings is itself a signal that something
upstream has gone wrong. The authors' own motivation for pygarble is
exactly this guardrail role: a cheap, local check that runs on every
prompt and every model output before anything more expensive does.

Two families of tools exist for the task. Trained classifiers, such as
the DistilBERT gibberish model used in open-source LLM guardrail
frameworks~\citep{madhurjindal}, are accurate on the distribution they
were trained on but need a model download and a deep-learning runtime,
and their decisions are hard to explain row by row. Rule-based detectors,
from character Markov models~\citep{gibberishdetector} to the nonsense
evaluator Nostril~\citep{hucka2018nostril} and the quality heuristics used
to filter web corpora~\citep{rae2021gopher,raffel2020t5}, are cheap,
local and explainable, and are often the first filter a team writes by
hand. What is rarely reported is how much junk such rules actually catch
on real data, and how much good text they wrongly flag.

This paper measures one such library. pygarble~\citep{pygarble} packages
\nstrategies{} strategies, each a function from a string to a score in
$[0,1]$, together with voting rules that combine them into profiles. It
has no runtime dependencies and ships its English reference tables inside
the wheel. We ask two plain questions. First, at default settings, on
data that played no part in setting its defaults, how much junk does it
capture, how much legitimate text does it flag, and what does it cost
compared with two common alternatives? Second, when a user has a small
labelled sample of their own data, how far can the same strategies be
tuned to it, and what do the learned weights say about which signals
matter?

The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\raggedright
\item A benchmark on two public datasets of short text, one of real
  human junk from survey research and one of random strings against
  tweets, with a frozen protocol, saved per-row predictions and a script
  that recomputes every reported number.
\item A comparison of pygarble's default and \code{english\_fusion}
  profiles with a character Markov-chain detector and a language
  identifier, all at their default decisions, on capture rate, precision,
  recall, false-positive rate, speed and install size.
\item A tuning study showing that a weight per strategy and a threshold,
  learned with Optuna from \tuningpct\,\% of the rows in seconds, lower
  the false-positive rate and raise precision on the held-out
  \heldoutpct\,\% in every draw, at the cost of some recall and with
  capture about level on average; the learned weights are reported in
  full, and the study is evaluated against the two profiles on identical
  rows and reported separately from the main results.
\item A description of pygarble's strategy families and profiles, with
  the full strategy list in the appendix.
\end{itemize}

\section{pygarble}
\label{sec:pygarble}

pygarble targets Python 3.8 and later and has no runtime dependencies.
The installed package, including its embedded English tables, is
\mb{english_fusion}\,MB. Besides gibberish detection it scans text for
secrets, personal data and profanity and can redact them; those features
are outside this paper.

\subsection{Strategies}

A \emph{strategy} is a function from a string to a score in $[0,1]$ and a
binary decision, with a flag that says whether the strategy applies to
the input at all. A strategy that needs letters or a minimum amount of
text, such as the Markov chain or cross parsing, reports that it does not
apply when the input has too little of either, rather than returning a
low score, so that absence of evidence is not counted as evidence that
the text is clean. The \nstrategies{} strategies fall into six families
(Table~\ref{tab:families}; the full list with one-line descriptions is in
Appendix~\ref{app:strategies}).

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\small
\caption{Strategy families in pygarble. Counts sum to \nstrategies{}.}
\label{tab:families}

\end{table}

\paragraph{Character statistics} measure properties of the character
stream that English rarely violates: the vowel ratio, Shannon entropy,
the longest consonant run, the letter-frequency profile, the symbol
share, and whether tokens are pronounceable.

\paragraph{English character models} compare the text with tables
learned from English: a character Markov chain over novel tokens, bigram
and trigram probabilities, a rare-trigram count, and a log-likelihood
ratio of English against a uniform reference.

\paragraph{Word-level checks} look words up in an embedded dictionary
and measure the unknown fraction, the fraction of anomalous tokens, the
density of English function words, the presence of English affixes, the
plausibility of adjacent word pairs, and whether the word-frequency
profile conforms to Zipf's law.

\paragraph{Structure and pattern checks} target forms of junk that are
not statistical: repeated substrings, keyboard-row walks and adjacent-key
sequences, hexadecimal strings, built-in patterns such as a letter
repeated four or more times or a five-key keyboard-row walk (with
optional user-supplied patterns), and local anomalies inside otherwise
ordinary text.

\paragraph{Encoding and script checks} fire on damage rather than on
meaning: mojibake byte signatures left by a wrong decoding, control
characters, and mixed or confusable Unicode scripts.

\paragraph{English-reference window statistics} compare a window of the
text with frequent English rather than applying a hand-written rule:
cross parsing~\citep{ziv1993crossparsing}, primed compression, $n$-gram
rank~\citep{cavnar1994ngram} and a permutation test. Each statistic is
measured against synthetic English of similar length,
and a score of 0.5 sits beyond the 99th percentile of that reference.

\subsection{Ensembles, voting and profiles}

\code{EnsembleDetector} runs a list of strategies and combines them
under a voting rule. With \code{any} voting, one applicable member
firing flags the text; \code{all}, \code{majority}, \code{average} and
\code{weighted} voting behave as their names say. \code{fisher} voting
treats each member's score as a test statistic: a table shipped with the
package records what that member scores on synthetic English word salads
of 8 to 480 characters, the member's $p$-value is the smallest tail
probability whose threshold the score exceeds, and the $p$-values are
combined with Fisher's method~\citep{fisher1925}. The combined $p$-value is mapped to a score so that the default
threshold of 0.5 flags a combined $p$-value of 0.001 or less. Named member
lists are called \emph{profiles}; Appendix~\ref{app:profiles} lists all
seven. Two are measured here:

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\raggedright
\item \code{english}, the default: Markov chain, log-likelihood ratio,
  word anomaly, mojibake, keyboard adjacency and control characters,
  under \code{any} voting. One member firing is enough.
\item \code{english\_fusion}, opt-in: word lookup, log-likelihood ratio
  and cross parsing under \code{fisher} voting. Each member's $p$-value is
  floored at 0.001, so when two or more members apply, at least two must
  score beyond their null thresholds before a text is flagged; one member
  decides alone only when it is the only one that applies.
\end{itemize}

A user can also pass any list of strategies, with a weight for each under
\code{weighted} voting, which is how the tuned configuration in
Section~\ref{sec:tuning} is built. The English profiles score
English plausibility; they are not language identifiers, and meaningful
text in other Latin-script languages can be flagged. Scores are heuristic,
not calibrated probabilities.

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{\SJ}

The Kaggle dataset \emph{Gibberish Text Classification}~\citep{dataA}
(hereafter \SJ{}) was compiled by a market-research practitioner from free-text responses
of poor-quality survey respondents. Its junk file holds \nArows{} rows,
every one labelled as junk; despite its \code{.xls} extension it is a
Windows-1252 CSV. We strip each response, drop empty rows and drop exact
duplicates, leaving \nA{} rows. Typical rows are keyboard mashes such as
runs of adjacent keys, repeated letters, and letters mixed with digits.
The median response is \Amedianchars{} characters long and
\Asingletokenpct\,\% are a single token.

Because the file has no clean rows, the only metric on this dataset is
the \emph{capture rate}: the share of rows a method flags. The licence is
listed as unknown on Kaggle, so the repository stores row indices and
the SHA-256 hash of each file, never the text.

\subsection{\TRS}

The Hugging Face dataset \emph{gibberish\_multilingual}~\citep{dataB}
(hereafter \TRS{}; Apache-2.0) pairs random character strings with
social-media posts in \nBlanguages{} languages. We pool its train, validation and test files and keep
the \nB{} non-empty rows whose language field is English: \nBpos{}
random strings, labelled junk, and \nBneg{} tweets, labelled clean.
Labels are used exactly as given and duplicates are kept (\nBdup{} rows
repeat an earlier text). The random strings are lower-case letter
sequences such as \code{edkylxhboa}; the tweets carry hashtags, handles
and, in \mojibakepct\,\% of cases, mojibake byte signatures from
UTF-8 text, mostly emoji and punctuation, decoded as Latin-1. The dataset labels those tweets clean
because it was built to separate random strings from posts.

On this dataset we report precision, recall and the false-positive rate
(FPR), the share of tweets flagged.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Methods.} pygarble 0.11.0 is used with its defaults frozen at
commit \code{e3135ed} (results were produced at \code{72b5b2e}, whose
package code is identical); no default or threshold was changed after any
result was seen. Two baselines run on CPU with pinned versions:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item \textbf{gibberish-detector} 0.1.1~\citep{gibberishdetector} (the
  Markov baseline below), a widely used character Markov model on PyPI.
  The package ships no model, so one is trained with its own
  trainer on the corpus its README uses, and a text is flagged when its
  score exceeds the command-line default of 4.0.
\item \textbf{lingua} 2.2.0~\citep{lingua}, a language identifier, under
  the policy ``not detected as English is junk''.
\end{itemize}
There are no neural baselines; the DistilBERT gibberish model was
excluded to keep the comparison to tools that run with no model download.

\paragraph{Decisions.} Every method is scored at its own default
decision, the binary output it gives with default settings. The
question is what each tool does as installed. The baselines expose no
tuning step short of retraining the Markov model, and the tuning study in
Section~\ref{sec:tuning} measures how far pygarble can be adapted with a
small sample, not whether it beats a re-tuned alternative. In the main
comparison no threshold is fitted on either dataset.

\paragraph{Intervals.} Uncertainty is given as 95\,\% percentile
intervals from \resamples{} bootstrap resamples of rows, drawn with a
fixed seed and shared by all methods.

\paragraph{Pilot and protocol changes.} Before the protocol was written,
both datasets were scored once with pygarble's defaults to judge
feasibility. The \code{english\_fusion} numbers from that pilot equal the
ones reported here, because nothing in the package changed afterwards.
The first version of the protocol also fitted thresholds at fixed
false-positive rates on a development split of the tweets dataset and
compared more baselines and pygarble configurations; both were computed
and then removed from the paper's scope (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
The tuning study in Section~\ref{sec:tuning} was added after the main
results were seen. It learns from labelled rows of both datasets and is
evaluated only on rows it did not see. Rows with identical text are kept
on the same side of the split, so no tweet in the held-out set also
appears in the tuning sample. Its threshold range was widened
once, from a lower bound of 0.2 to \tuninglow{}, and its budget raised
from 400 to \tuningtrials{} trials, because every draw of a first run
chose the bound and found its best trial late. That decision rested on
the tuning samples; the first run's held-out results had also been
computed, and they are kept in the repository history. The tuning sample
was at first drawn row by row, which let copies of a repeated tweet fall
on both sides of the split; the study was re-run with the grouped split,
and the earlier results, which were slightly more favourable to the tuned
configuration, are recorded in the deviations log.

\paragraph{Environment.} All runs are single-threaded on an \cpuname{}
with Python \pyversion{}. Hardware, package versions and the pygarble
commit are recorded with the results.

Table~\ref{tab:survey} and Figure~\ref{fig:results} give the capture
rates. \code{english\_fusion}
flags \respct{english_fusion}{A_capture}\,\% of the junk responses
(\resci{english_fusion}{A_capture}), and the default \code{english}
profile \respct{english}{A_capture}\,\%. The Markov baseline captures
\respct{gibberish_detector}{A_capture}\,\%, and lingua, which flags
anything it does not identify as English, captures
\respct{lingua}{A_capture}\,\%.

The ordering follows what each method can see. Keyboard mashes and
random letters have improbable character transitions and no dictionary
words, so every method catches most of them. Lingua's near-complete
capture comes from the policy rather than from junk detection: a
short mash is not confidently English, so it is flagged, and
Section~\ref{sec:tweets} shows what that policy costs on real text. The
Markov baseline misses the rows whose transitions happen to be plausible,
such as repeated syllables. The \nAmissedfusion{} rows that
\code{english\_fusion} misses are mostly one to three characters long,
letters mixed with digits, strings with no letters at all, or a short
syllable or punctuation mark repeated; some are real words or short
phrases, which read as English to character and word statistics.

The dataset is used as labelled, and it has some label noise:
\nAmissedall{} rows are flagged by none of the four methods, and
on inspection many are meaningful low-effort answers such as a thanks or
a short positive phrase. The capture rates therefore have a ceiling
below one.

The profiles above are fixed member lists. A user with a labelled
sample of their own data can instead learn how much each strategy should count. We
learned a weight in $[0,1]$ for each of the \nstrategies{} strategies,
where 0 switches a strategy off, and a threshold in
$[\tuninglow,\tuninghigh]$, under pygarble's \code{weighted} voting: a
text's score is the weighted mean of the scores of the members that apply
to it, and the text is flagged when the score reaches the threshold.
Weights move in steps of \tuningweightstep{} and the threshold in steps
of 0.01.

The tuning sample is a stratified \tuningpct\,\% of each dataset:
\nTuneA{} \SJ{} rows and \nTuneB{} \TRS{} rows in proportion to their
labels. Rows with identical text are kept on the same side of the split,
so no tweet in the held-out set also appears in the tuning sample.
Optuna~\citep{optuna} with its tree-structured Parzen estimator
sampler~\citep{bergstra2011tpe}, with a fixed seed, searched
\tuningtrials{} configurations for the best F1 on that sample, with junk
as the positive class. The search was still improving when it stopped:
in every draw the best configuration came after trial \tuningbestmin{}
of \tuningtrials{}, so a larger budget may fit the tuning sample better. Every strategy is scored once per row and cached
(\tuningscoring{} seconds for both datasets), so the search takes about
\tuningseconds{} seconds per sample on one core. The draw is repeated with
\tuningdraws{} seeds, \tuningseed{} to \tuningseedlast{}, the first being
the primary draw. Each learned configuration was rebuilt as an
\code{EnsembleDetector} and run on the held-out \heldoutpct\,\%, where it
reproduced the cached decisions on every row, and the two profiles were
scored on the same rows.

Table~\ref{tab:tuning} gives the results. On the primary draw the tuned
configuration flags \trespct{tuned}{B_fpr}\,\% of held-out tweets
(\tresci{tuned}{B_fpr}) against \trespct{english_fusion}{B_fpr}\,\% for
\code{english\_fusion} and \trespct{english}{B_fpr}\,\% for
\code{english}. It captures \trespct{tuned}{A_capture}\,\% of held-out
survey junk against \trespct{english_fusion}{A_capture}\,\%, and recalls
\trespct{tuned}{B_recall}\,\% of the random strings against
\trespct{english_fusion}{B_recall}\,\%. Over the \tuningdraws{} draws its
false-positive rate is \tmeanpct{tuned}{B_fpr}\,\% (sample standard deviation
\tsdpct{tuned}{B_fpr} points) against \tmeanpct{english_fusion}{B_fpr}\,\%,
its capture \tmeanpct{tuned}{A_capture}\,\% against
\tmeanpct{english_fusion}{A_capture}\,\%, and its recall
\tmeanpct{tuned}{B_recall}\,\% against
\tmeanpct{english_fusion}{B_recall}\,\%. The gain over
\code{english\_fusion} is in false positives and precision, which are
better in every draw; capture is about equal on average and recall is
lower. The tuned capture is below the profile's in
\tuningcapturelower{} of the \tuningdraws{} draws and the tuned recall in
\tuningrecalllower{}, and the primary draw is one where both are lower:
its recall interval lies wholly below the profile's. Across the draws,
recall was as low as \tuningworstrecall\,\% and capture as low as
\tuningworstcapture\,\%. The tuned rows vary more between draws than the
profiles, whose only variation is which rows are held out, because a
\tuningpct\,\% sample is small enough for the learned weights to differ
from draw to draw.

Between \tuningmembersmin{} and \tuningmembersmax{} of the
\nstrategies{} strategies kept a non-zero weight, and the thresholds lie
between \tuningthresholdmin{} and \tuningthresholdmax{}. The largest mean
weights went to \tuningtop{}; the first had full weight in every draw,
while the others moved between draws. The smallest went to
\tuningbottom{}; no strategy was switched off in every draw, and in the
primary draw \tuningoff{} have weight zero. Learning from the labels alone, the search
thus gave almost no weight to the two encoding-damage checks, which fire
on tweets that \TRS{} labels clean. Appendix~\ref{app:weights} lists every weight.

A user reproduces this on their own data by scoring a labelled sample
with each strategy, searching weights and a threshold (the benchmark's
\code{tune.py} does this with Optuna), and building the detector from the
strategies with non-zero weight:
\begin{small}
\begin{verbatim}
EnsembleDetector(strategies=names,
                 weights=weights,
                 voting="weighted",
                 threshold=threshold)
\end{verbatim}
\end{small}
The package itself is unchanged; the weights describe these two datasets,
not junk in general.

install size. pygarble scores a thousand short texts in
\ms{english_fusion}\,ms with \code{english\_fusion} and installs in
\mb{english_fusion}\,MB with nothing else. The Markov baseline is the
fastest, at \ms{gibberish_detector}\,ms, with a \mb{gibberish_detector}\,MB
install that includes its training corpus.
Lingua needs \ms{lingua}\,ms and a \mb{lingua}\,MB install that bundles
its language models. All
three run offline once installed. Timings depend on the machine.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the results support.} Two things. First, as shipped,
pygarble's \code{english\_fusion} profile captures about nine in ten real
survey junk responses and nearly every random string while flagging about
one tweet in fifteen, from a one-megabyte install with no dependencies.
Neither baseline both captures more survey junk and flags fewer tweets: the Markov baseline
flags far fewer tweets but misses more than a quarter of the survey junk,
and lingua captures almost all junk but flags more tweets and needs a
three-hundred-megabyte install. Second, the ensemble is tunable from a
small labelled sample in seconds, though tuning trades recall for
precision rather than improving every endpoint. The learned weights lower
the tweet false-positive rate from \tmeanpct{english_fusion}{B_fpr}\,\%
to \tmeanpct{tuned}{B_fpr}\,\%, below the profile's in each of the
\tuningdraws{} draws, and keep capture about level on average
(\tmeanpct{tuned}{A_capture}\,\% against
\tmeanpct{english_fusion}{A_capture}\,\%), but recall falls
(\tmeanpct{tuned}{B_recall}\,\% against
\tmeanpct{english_fusion}{B_recall}\,\%). The draws vary: on the primary
draw both capture and recall are below the profile's. The weights explain
the result at the extremes:
\tuningtop{} get the largest mean weights, and \tuningbottom{} get little
or none; for the two encoding checks that fits the dataset labelling
encoding damage as clean. Individual weights vary widely between draws,
so only the extremes are stable. A user who disagrees with that
labelling turns those checks back on; nothing is hidden inside a model.

\paragraph{Where pygarble is beaten.} The Markov baseline has the lowest
false-positive rate on tweets, \respct{gibberish_detector}{B_fpr}\,\%
against \respct{english_fusion}{B_fpr}\,\%; when wrongly flagging a real
post costs much more than missing junk, it is the better default. Lingua
captures more survey junk, \respct{lingua}{A_capture}\,\% against
\respct{english_fusion}{A_capture}\,\%, under its aggressive policy. The
default \code{english} profile is not suitable for informal text as
shipped: it flags a third of tweets, its encoding checks
\englishencodingpct\,\% and its Markov member alone \englishmarkovpct\,\%.

\paragraph{Guidance.} For screening survey or form free text with no
labelled data, use \code{english\_fusion}; add a minimum-length rule if
one- and two-character answers count as junk. With a labelled sample of
the target domain (the study used \nTune{} rows), learn a weight per
strategy and a threshold as in Section~\ref{sec:tuning}: it takes seconds,
needs no model, and gave a lower false-positive rate and higher precision
than \code{english\_fusion} in every draw, but lower recall in
\tuningrecalllower{} of \tuningdraws{}, so check recall on held-out
labelled rows before adopting the learned weights. Without labels, start from a profile and remove the
strategies whose target the domain does not treat as junk. When false positives are costly,
prefer the Markov baseline at default settings, or measure a threshold on
labelled rows of your own data. For multilingual input, route by language
first; a language identifier answers that question, and it is not a junk
detector.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Both datasets are English and short. We measure how much labelled junk
is caught and how many real tweets are wrongly flagged; we do not measure
behaviour on clean survey answers, bot spam, other languages or long
documents, and the results should not be read beyond that scope. \SJ{} has only junk, so it
measures capture and nothing about false positives; \TRS{} has random
strings as its only junk, which are easier than real junk. There are two
baselines and no neural one. The main comparison runs every method at its
default decision, so it says how the tools behave out of the box. Only
pygarble is tuned; a tuned baseline might narrow the gap. The tuning
study was added after the main results were seen and learns from labelled
rows of both datasets; its evaluation rows are held out, but its samples
come from the same two datasets, so the learned weights fit them and not
junk in general. A comparison at a matched
false-positive rate, with thresholds fitted on a development split, was
computed in an earlier version of the protocol and removed from the
paper's scope; in it the Markov baseline captured more survey junk than
\code{english\_fusion}. It remains in the repository history.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

The benchmark code, the frozen protocol with its dated deviations file,
and the results are in the repository under \code{paper/}. The manifest
records the SHA-256 hash of each raw file (for \SJ{}, beginning
\code{\shaA}) and every row id; no dataset text is committed. Per-row
scores and decisions for every method are committed, so every number in
this paper can be recomputed without the raw data:
\begin{small}
\begin{verbatim}
make benchmark-verify PYTHON=<venv>/bin/python
make preprint PYTHON=<venv>/bin/python
\end{verbatim}
\end{small}
The first command recomputes the metrics from the saved predictions, and
the tuning results from the cached strategy scores, the recorded tuning
rows and the learned weights, and fails on any difference. The second regenerates the figure, the tables
and the number macros this paper is typeset from, then builds the PDF.
Re-scoring from the raw files is described in the benchmark README;
scores are deterministic.
